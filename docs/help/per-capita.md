# Per capita (-p)

GDP, debt and interest drawn per person: each divided by the nation's population on the same date, in its own currency. The right axis reads "Nominal USD per Capita (Log Scale)", in each nation's currency, and each line's name ends "per Capita".

## How the division is made

Each figure is divided when it is observed, by the population on that date, and held until the next figure, so the lines keep their steps: a debt reported once a year stays level until the next year's figure, instead of drifting down through the year as the population grows. The population between two estimates is drawn on a straight line on a log scale (steady growth from one to the next); after the last estimate it is held; before the first there is none, and nothing is drawn.

Per capita can't be combined with [As % of GDP](?help=percent-of-gdp): a curve is either an amount per person or a share of GDP, and in the window and on the page ticking one unticks the other. It combines with [Inflation adjusted](?help=constant-prices), for real amounts per person. The [Population](?help=population) box draws the figures themselves. Without [GDP](?help=gdp), [debt](?help=debt) or [interest](?help=interest) ticked it has no effect, and its box is greyed.

## Where each population comes from

- **Canada, 1867 to 1945.** The estimated population on 1 June of each year, Statistics Canada's historical table [17-10-0063](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/t1/tbl1/en/tv.action?pid=1710006301), archived.
- **Canada, 1946 on.** Statistics Canada's quarterly population estimates, table [17-10-0009](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/t1/tbl1/en/tv.action?pid=1710000901), on the first day of each quarter; the release of 2026-09-23 brought 1 July 2026. The two differ by about 0.2 % where both exist, mostly the month between their dates, so they are laid end to end without scaling.
- **The United States, 1947 on.** The Bureau of Economic Analysis' population, the one it divides by for its own figures per person: the resident population plus the armed forces overseas, quarterly from 1947, in thousands ([B230RC0Q173SBEA on FRED](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/B230RC0Q173SBEA)). FRED had the second quarter of 2026 on 2026-07-30; the next release is on 2026-10-29. U.S. GDP and interest begin in 1947 and debt in 1966, so nothing earlier is needed.
- **The United Kingdom, 1707 to 1954.** The Bank of England's dataset "A millennium of macroeconomic data", version 3.1 ([research datasets](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/statistics/research-datasets)), at 30 June of each year: Great Britain to 1800, with all of Ireland from 1801 to 1921, and with Northern Ireland alone from 1922. The fall in 1922 is Irish independence.
- **The United Kingdom, 1955 on.** The UK resident population, the mid-year estimates interpolated to quarters, [EBAQ at the ONS](https://www.ons.gov.uk/economy/grossdomesticproductgdp/timeseries/ebaq/ukea), in thousands, each quarter dated by its middle. The release of 2026-09-30 brought the second quarter of 2026; the next is on 2026-12-22. It agrees with the Bank's figure where they meet (50.95 million in mid-1955).
- **Germany, 1950 to 1959.** West Germany's average population in each year, with the Saarland and West Berlin throughout, in thousands, each year dated 1 July: the Bundesbank's [Lange Zeitreihen](https://www.bundesbank.de/resource/blob/846346/6bfc076ef91cdb9d0600f257da83ca28/mL/2020-10-02-12-56-47-lange-zeitreihen-data.pdf) of 2020, part V, table 1. It meets the count on 1 January 1960 within about 0.2 % (54.88 million in 1959, 55.26 million then), so the two are laid end to end without scaling.
- **Germany, 1960 to 1990.** The population on 1 January of each year, Eurostat's dataset [demo_pjan](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/databrowser/view/demo_pjan/default/table?lang=en): West Germany until 1990, all of Germany from 1991, a step of 17 million at unification.
- **Germany, 1991 on.** The total population in the national accounts' sense, Eurostat's dataset [namq_10_pe](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/databrowser/view/namq_10_pe/default/table?lang=en), quarterly, in thousands, each quarter dated by its middle; the second quarter of 2026 is the latest.
- **West Germany's GDP, 1950 to 1959: its own population.** That GDP leaves out the Saarland and West Berlin, which the population above has, and divided by it would read about 6 % low and jump at 1960. The Federal Statistical Office publishes the GDP per person of that same territory in the [same table](?help=gdp), and those figures are drawn as published. From 1960 the GDP is whole, and the population above applies.
- **West Germany's GDP, 1990 and 1991: its own population.** The population above reaches all of Germany's on 1 January 1991, running up to it through 1990, while West Germany's GDP goes on to 1991-09-30, and divided by it would read up to 20 % low. So those figures are divided by West Germany's yearly averages from the same Lange Zeitreihen (63.25 million in 1990, 64.07 million in 1991), on a straight line on a log scale between their middles, 1991's held after its own. With all of Germany's GDP at the end of 1991 the population above applies again, so the curve falls at unification, from about 21,700 to about 19,000 euros, East Germany's GDP per person being much the lower.
- **West Germany's debt: the same two spans.** To 1959 the Federal Statistical Office's own figures per person, from the [debt's table](?help=debt), are drawn as published, 190 euros at 1950-03-31 and 462 at 1959-03-31. The Office divides by the people of the whole territory throughout, 50.3 million in 1950, though the debt leaves out West Berlin until 1952 and the Saarland until 1960, so neither is a step here. Each level is over the same people, so the levels add up; the Office's own column for local government is over a smaller population (46.05 million in 1950), so it is not the figure drawn. From 1960 the population above applies, except 1990's figure, divided by the same West German population as the GDP.
- **West Germany's interest: 1990 its own population.** Its years begin in 1970, when the population above is West Germany's. 1990's figure, at the end of that year, is divided by West Germany's average on that day, as the GDP's is, not by the population above, which by then has run up most of the way to all of Germany's and would make it about 20 % low. All of Germany's from 1991 is over the population above. Each level the same.

## How the program keeps these figures

Each source's figures are kept in the program as the source published them. A job on GitHub asks every live source each day and adds the new figures; a revision of older ones waits for review before it is taken. When a chart is drawn, the program takes only the newest figures from each source and draws the older ones from what it holds: those from 24 months before the last figure it holds where the source revises its figures (GDP, debt, interest, population and the mortgage rates from surveys), from two months before where it doesn't (the yields, and the rates that are set or traded). If a source doesn't answer, the chart is drawn from what the program holds, with a warning naming the source and its last date. The sources that have stopped (Statistics Canada's old tables, Historical Statistics of Canada, the Bank of Canada's historical tables, the Bank of England's dataset, and the series the Federal Reserve, the Bundesbank and the ECB no longer update) are kept as they were last published, and asked nothing.
